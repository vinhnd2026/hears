What makes an environment harsh for a scale?

A plain-language definition of harsh and hazardous environments for industrial weighing: washdown, flooding, corrosion, lightning, dust and explosive atmospheres — and what each one does to a scale.

Last updated: 2026-11-18

A working definition

An environment is harsh for a scale when the conditions at the weighing point attack the scale itself — its sensors, cables and electronics — and not just the product being weighed. A scale in a clean, dry warehouse lives an easy life. A scale that is hosed down every night, stands in a pit that floods, breathes corrosive vapour or takes lightning strikes through long cable runs lives a harsh one, and it usually wears out quickly if it was designed for the warehouse.

Hazardous is the stricter word. A hazardous area is one where an explosive atmosphere — flammable gas, vapour or combustible dust — may be present, so equipment must be selected and installed under the site's hazardous-area classification. Harsh describes what the environment does to the equipment; hazardous describes what the equipment must never do to the environment — provide a spark or a hot surface. Many real sites are both.

1. Washdown and flooding

Food, beverage and seafood plants wash equipment down daily, often with high-pressure water and cleaning chemicals. Water finds cable glands, junction boxes and the inside of electronic load cells. Pit scales add flooding: a pit that fills with wash water or storm water submerges everything installed in it.

2. Corrosion and hygiene

Chemical plants, fertiliser stores and farm buildings expose metal and electronics to corrosive vapour — on farms, ammonia from livestock attacks copper, brass and plated parts whenever moisture is present. Hygienic production adds its own demand: surfaces must be stainless, smooth and cleanable, with nowhere for product or bacteria to lodge.

3. Lightning and electrical surges

Outdoor scales — weighbridges, tank and silo scales — are tall metal structures on open ground, connected to the control room by long copper cables. A nearby lightning strike travels along those cables and destroys load cells and indicators. Vietnam's rainy season makes this a routine risk, not an exception.

4. Dust, vibration and heavy traffic

Quarries, cement and steel yards combine abrasive dust, constant vibration and heavy trucks passing around the clock. Dust that is combustible — grain, feed, coal and some chemical powders — also turns an ordinary harsh site into a hazardous one, because the dust cloud itself can explode.

5. Hazardous areas: gas and dust

Where flammable gas, vapour or combustible dust may occur, the site is classified into zones, and every electrical device in the zone must be certified for it. That classification is specific to each plant and must be confirmed by the plant's own assessment — it cannot be assumed from a brochure. The practical question for weighing is simple: how much electrical equipment has to sit inside the zone at all?

6. Heat, humidity and hard access

Tropical heat and humidity accelerate every failure above, and many weighing points — under a silo, inside a pit, on top of a tank — are hard to reach, so a failed sensor is expensive to replace even when the part itself is cheap.

What to tell HearS

When you ask HearS about weighing in a harsh environment, tell us: what is weighed, how the area is cleaned and with what chemicals, whether the pit or floor floods, whether the site is classified as hazardous, and how often scales have failed there before. Those five answers decide the technology — and they are why HearS starts every harsh-environment project with a site survey.
